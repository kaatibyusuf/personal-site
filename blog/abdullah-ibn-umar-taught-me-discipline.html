abdullah ibn umar taught me discipline

25 November 2026 · 12 min read

about ten years ago, i was fortunate to read about abdullah ibn umar, radiyallahu anhuma, the son of umar ibn al-khattab. his life left an impression on me, and i have returned to it many times since.

i used to think discipline was synonymous with being hard on yourself. waking up at four, keeping no excuses, taking cold showers, and following a schedule so rigid that even the slightest deviation felt like failure. i tried that version of discipline several times. it would work for a few weeks, sometimes longer, until i exhausted myself and discovered that being unnecessarily harsh with yourself is not the same as being disciplined.

studying the life of abdullah ibn umar offered a more enduring understanding.

his discipline was not defined by theatrics, extraordinary declarations or an obsession with appearing productive. it was visible in his decisions, his worship, his restraint, his learning, his generosity and his remarkable consistency over decades.

there are lessons in his life that every person serious about building a meaningful life ought to consider.

1. he was turned back, but he remained ready.

when the muslims marched towards uhud, abdullah ibn umar was fourteen years old. he presented himself to the prophet ﷺ, hoping to participate in the battle, but was turned back because of his age.

a year later, at the battle of the trench, he presented himself again. he was fifteen, and this time he was accepted.

there is a lesson here about how one responds to rejection, delay and limitations.

many people allow a single disappointment to redefine their ambitions. they are denied an opportunity and immediately begin to question whether they were ever meant for it. rather than using the interval to improve themselves, they become consumed by the disappointment of not having been selected, accepted or permitted to proceed.

abdullah ibn umar returned.

discipline is not merely the ability to pursue an ambition when circumstances are favourable. it is the ability to remain prepared when circumstances are not. being denied something today does not absolve you of the responsibility to prepare for tomorrow.

if an opportunity is beyond your present capacity, develop the capacity. if you are too young, inexperienced or unqualified, address those limitations. if you must wait, wait without allowing your character to deteriorate in the process.

a rejection should not make you less prepared for the next opportunity.

2. one sentence changed his nights.

this is one of the most remarkable accounts of abdullah ibn umar's life.

abdullah ibn umar used to sleep in the mosque during the time of the prophet ﷺ. when people had dreams, they would recount them to the prophet ﷺ. abdullah once dreamt that two angels carried him towards the fire, before another angel met them and reassured him. his sister hafsah subsequently narrated the dream to the prophet ﷺ.

the prophet ﷺ then said:

"abdullah is a good man, if only he would pray at night."

salim ibn abdullah later reported that after this, abdullah slept very little at night.

consider the response. he received counsel concerning one aspect of his life, and he acted upon it. he did not merely appreciate the compliment, dismiss the correction or postpone the necessary change. he responded with action.

one of the greatest impediments to personal development is our tendency to become comfortable with advice we have no intention of implementing. we collect counsel, attend lectures, read books, listen to reminders and discuss improvement, yet our lives remain largely unchanged.

knowledge of what needs to be done is not the same as doing it.

sometimes, the most consequential improvement you can make to your life is not introducing ten new routines. it is finally addressing one deficiency you have repeatedly been confronted with.

when you identify a weakness, do not romanticise the process of recognising it. correct it. when sound advice reaches you, do not become so occupied with discussing its wisdom that you neglect its application.

3. he spent eight years learning sūratul-baqarah.

there is a report in the muwatta of imam malik that abdullah ibn umar spent eight years learning sūratul-baqarah.

eight years.

the report is often understood in the context of how the companions learnt the qur'an. they did not merely pursue memorisation, but studied the verses, their meanings, their implications and their obligations, putting what they learnt into practice before moving forward.

this is a sharp contrast to the culture of hurried acquisition in which we live. people want to finish books, obtain certificates, complete courses and accumulate skills, often without giving any of those things sufficient time to transform their conduct.

the problem is not the desire to learn quickly. the problem is mistaking speed for depth and completion for mastery.

there is little value in reading a hundred books if none of them improves your judgment. there is little value in completing course after course if you cannot apply what you have learnt. there is little value in accumulating knowledge that remains detached from your decisions, character and responsibilities.

learning should produce a visible difference in the person who undertakes it.

before beginning another course, acquiring another book or pursuing another skill, ask yourself whether you have sufficiently applied what you already know. sometimes, the next level of your development is not another resource. it is the faithful application of the one you already possess.

4. he loved the footsteps of the prophet ﷺ.

abdullah ibn umar was known for his remarkable attentiveness to the way the prophet ﷺ lived. he would observe where the prophet ﷺ prayed, where he rested during journeys and the places he visited, sometimes recreating these actions in his own life.

his devotion to following the prophet ﷺ became a distinguishing feature of his conduct.

this illustrates something important about discipline. discipline is easier to understand when it is connected to something greater than personal ambition.

abdullah ibn umar was not merely interested in establishing a routine. he was deeply committed to following the example of the prophet ﷺ. his attention to the prophet's conduct reflected a devotion that extended beyond admiration into action.

this is what genuine admiration ought to produce.

when you admire someone, pay attention to what makes them admirable. study their principles, their conduct, their decisions and the habits that sustain their character. do not reduce admiration to praise, imitation for appearances or an endless discussion of what makes someone exceptional.

love ought to produce attention, and attention ought to produce action.

the same principle applies to our personal development. habits sustained by purpose are often stronger than habits sustained by guilt. when you understand why something matters, you are better positioned to remain committed to it, even when the excitement of beginning has disappeared.

5. he lived like a traveller.

the prophet ﷺ once took abdullah ibn umar by the shoulder and said:

"be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveller."

abdullah ibn umar took this counsel seriously. he would say:

"when you reach the evening, do not expect to reach the morning, and when you reach the morning, do not expect to reach the evening. take from your health for your sickness, and from your life for your death."

this is one of the most practical principles of discipline.

people waste enormous portions of their lives because they behave as though their resources are inexhaustible. they postpone important work, delay necessary conversations, neglect their health and treat their ambitions as though there will always be another season to pursue them.

but time is not replenished. health is not guaranteed. opportunities do not always return.

take from your health for your sickness. take from your life for your death. build while you have the strength to build. learn while your mind is capable of learning. give while you have something to give. strengthen your relationships while the people you love are still within reach.

a traveller does not confuse a temporary station for a permanent residence. he understands that his time there is limited, and that understanding informs what he does with it.

we ought to approach our lives with the same awareness. the knowledge that our time is limited should not make us paralysed with fear. it should make us more deliberate about what deserves our time.

6. he knew when to say no.

abdullah ibn umar was known for his caution in matters of religious judgment. he was reportedly offered the position of judge by uthman ibn affan, radiyallahu anhu, but declined. he was careful about issuing religious verdicts and was not ashamed to admit when he did not know something.

during the turbulent period of civil strife among the muslims, he was also notably cautious about participating in the fighting.

i will leave the historical and scholarly discussions surrounding these events to those better qualified to examine them. what is relevant here is his restraint.

he was a man of considerable standing, knowledge and influence. yet he did not regard every position, opportunity or invitation as something he must accept. he understood that possessing the ability to undertake something did not necessarily mean it was his responsibility to do so.

one of the most expensive mistakes a person can make is accepting every opportunity without considering what it will displace.

not every invitation deserves your attendance. not every project deserves your commitment. not every position is worth occupying. not every argument requires your participation. not every opportunity is consistent with the responsibilities you already have.

many people are not struggling because they lack opportunities. they are struggling because they have not learnt to exercise judgment over the opportunities they accept.

discipline also means knowing your limits, declining what you cannot responsibly carry, saying i do not know when you do not know, and refusing to allow the possibility of doing something to become an obligation to do it.

7. he did not allow wealth to own him.

abdullah ibn umar was known for his generosity. reports about his life describe his giving, his freeing of slaves and his willingness to part with things he loved for the sake of Allah. he was also known for his hospitality, frequently ensuring that the poor and orphans were present at his meals.

what is remarkable is not merely the amount he gave, but the disposition with which he approached what he possessed.

he had wealth, yet wealth did not appear to be the centre around which his life revolved.

we often associate discipline with restraint. we think of it as the ability to resist temptation, preserve resources, control impulses and prevent ourselves from being carried away by our desires. but discipline is also the ability to release what you love when keeping it would compromise something more important.

it is one thing to refrain from acquiring what you do not particularly desire. it is another to willingly part with something you genuinely love.

a person who cannot give without resentment, who cannot part with possessions without anxiety and who measures every act of generosity against what it costs them has not necessarily mastered their relationship with wealth.

wealth, time, possessions and opportunities are all trusts. the objective is not to become so attached to possessing them that you forget their purpose.

learn to acquire responsibly, preserve wisely and give generously.

8. he came from a house that taught accountability.

abdullah ibn umar was raised in the household of umar ibn al-khattab, radiyallahu anhu, a man widely associated with vigilance, accountability and seriousness about his responsibilities.

umar is attributed with the counsel:

"take account of yourselves before you are taken to account."

this is one of the foundations of discipline. a person who refuses to examine their conduct will eventually become accustomed to their own deficiencies. mistakes become habits, negligence becomes normalised and excuses gradually replace accountability.

self-accountability is not self-punishment. it is an honest assessment of one's conduct, decisions, responsibilities and priorities.

what did you accomplish today? what did you neglect? where did you act irresponsibly? what needs correcting? what must you do differently tomorrow?

a person who intends to live deliberately must develop the habit of examining their life. you cannot consistently improve what you are unwilling to evaluate.

i recommend a simple practice at the end of each day. ask yourself three questions:

what did i do today for my lord?

what did i do today for the people i love?

what must i change tomorrow?

these questions are simple enough to answer in a few minutes, but their purpose is to prevent us from living without conscious direction. a few minutes of honest reflection can expose the difference between the life we claim to prioritise and the life our decisions are actually producing.

what we ought to take from him.

when i consider the life of abdullah ibn umar, i see a man who took his responsibilities seriously. his discipline was not confined to one aspect of his life. it was evident in his worship, his learning, his restraint, his generosity, his adherence to the sunnah and his relationship with the world.

there are principles worth taking from his life:

  • when you are turned back, remain ready.
  • when you are corrected, act upon the correction.
  • stay with what you are learning long enough for it to change you.
  • let love inform your discipline.
  • remember that your time and health are temporary.
  • learn to say no, and learn to admit when you do not know.
  • give freely of what you love.
  • examine yourself before the day ends.

abdullah ibn umar lived for decades after the death of the prophet ﷺ, and his life was remembered for his devotion, knowledge, restraint, generosity and remarkable adherence to the sunnah.

discipline is not built through occasional enthusiasm or dramatic resolutions. it is established through decisions made repeatedly, responsibilities upheld consistently and corrections implemented without unnecessary delay.

we ought to stop romanticising discipline and start practising it. our worship should reflect it. our learning should reflect it. our relationships, finances, work and commitments should reflect it.

may Allah be pleased with abdullah ibn umar, and may he grant us the ability to live with the sincerity, restraint, devotion and consistency that made his life worth remembering.
