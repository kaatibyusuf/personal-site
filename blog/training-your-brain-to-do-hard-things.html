training your brain to do hard things

28 November 2026 · 8 min read

the first thing you should know about bilal ibn rabah, radiyallahu anhu, is that he was not born brave.

he was a slave in makkah, owning nothing, not even his own body. when he accepted islam, his master umayyah ibn khalaf did what the powerful do to people who step out of line. according to ibn ishaq, umayyah would bring him out at the hottest part of the day, throw him on his back in the open valley and have a great rock put on his chest, telling him he would stay there until he died or denied. bilal kept repeating "ahad, ahad," until abu bakr as-siddiq, radiyallahu anhu, took pity on him and bought his freedom. after the migration to madinah, he became the first mu'adhdhin.

we read that story with awe, and the awe keeps us at a distance. we file bilal under a different species of man and move on. i want to argue the opposite. courage like his is built, and the materials are the same ones available to you tonight: a reason, a word, a small rule, a little practice, and a friend.

the price and the prize

before you do anything difficult, a quiet calculation runs in your head: what will this cost, and what is it worth?

neuroscientists have a candidate for where that calculation happens. a review by touroutoglou and colleagues argues that the anterior mid-cingulate cortex is an important network hub that performs the cost-benefit computations necessary for tenacity, which is persistence in the face of challenge. faced with a challenge such as mastering complex equations or training for a marathon, many people find the effort too costly and withdraw.

withdrawal, then, is often arithmetic. the cost looks larger than the prize. bilal's arithmetic came out differently, because the prize was not a comfortable life. it was the truth he had found, and no rock could be heavy enough to outweigh it.

this is the first law of doing hard things: raise the prize before you try to lower the cost. a weak reason will lose to a strong difficulty every time. ask what this is worth to you, write the answer in a sentence, and refuse to begin until the sentence holds weight.

one word

under the stone, bilal did not give a speech. he repeated one word: ahad. one.

that is a technique with a research record. a meta-analysis of 32 studies and 62 effect sizes found a positive, moderate effect (ES = .48) of self-talk interventions on sport performance. the strategy works through self-addressed cues, words or small phrases, that trigger appropriate responses by focusing attention and psyching up.

a mind in distress produces a flood of arguments for stopping. a single word gives it something to hold instead of something to debate. bilal had no laboratory, yet he had the method: when everything is shouting, say the one thing that matters, and say it again.

choose your word before the hard moment arrives. it should point at your reason, not at your fear. when the urge to quit comes, say it, then take the next small step.

a rule with a trigger

the most useful thing i know about bilal is not the sand. it is a sentence he spoke to the prophet ﷺ.

in sahih al-bukhari, at the time of the fajr prayer, the prophet ﷺ asked bilal to tell him of the best deed he had done after embracing islam, because he had heard his footsteps in front of him in paradise. bilal replied that he had not done anything worth mentioning except that whenever he performed ablution, during the day or night, he prayed after that ablution as much as was written for him. the narration is also in sahih muslim.

study the structure. nothing in it is dramatic. it is a rule with a trigger: when i finish wudu, i pray. day or night, every time, with no exceptions.

psychologists call this an implementation intention, an if-then plan, and the evidence for it is strong. a meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (d = .65) on goal attainment, helping people initiate goal striving, shield it from unwanted influences, and disengage from failing courses of action.

the value of an if-then plan lies in when the decision gets made. you make it in advance, in a calm hour, and the trigger carries it out at the moment when your resistance is highest. bilal never had to decide, each time, whether to pray after wudu. the question was closed years earlier.

build yours the same way. after fajr, i open the book. after i close the shop, i walk for twenty minutes. after wudu, i pray two rak'ahs. tiny, certain, and repeated.

how long it takes

repetition does the real work, and repetition takes time. in a well-known study by phillippa lally and colleagues, 96 undergraduates adopted a new daily behaviour linked to a cue for 84 days. the average time to reach maximum automaticity was 66 days, ranging from 18 to a predicted 254 days. the median was 66, and observation ended after 84 days, so longer times were extrapolated.

the range is the lesson. some habits lock in within weeks, others take most of a year. progress that feels slow is still progress, and a person who needs 200 days is not weaker than one who needs 20. both are doing the same thing: showing up at the same cue until the effort stops feeling like effort.

the empty tank

for years, popular psychology taught that willpower is a fuel tank that runs dry with use. the theory was called ego depletion, and it shaped a decade of self-help advice.

then the field tested it at scale. a multilab preregistered replication, using 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants, found that the ego-depletion effect was small, with confidence intervals that encompassed zero (d = 0.04). the tank, at least in the form it was sold to us, did not hold up.

that fits what bilal's life shows. he did not endure on a reserve that happened to be large enough. he endured because the arithmetic in his heart was settled, and settled arithmetic does not run out.

stronger by doing

nobody goes from a quiet life to the sand. psychologist albert bandura built his theory of self-efficacy on a simple observation, and according to bandura, the most effective way to build self-efficacy is through mastery experiences, personal experiences of success. achieving difficult goals in the face of adversity builds confidence and strengthens perseverance.

the belief that you can do hard things comes from having done them, in doses small enough to finish.

the brain itself changes with sustained practice. in a 2000 study of london taxi drivers, who spend about two years learning the city's streets, the posterior hippocampi of the drivers were significantly larger than those of control subjects. hippocampal volume correlated with the time spent as a taxi driver. a later study followed trainees through their years of learning: those who qualified showed an increase in gray matter volume in the posterior hippocampus. years of hard practice leave a physical mark on the organ that does the practising.

the prophet ﷺ defined strength in a way that belongs in any conversation about discipline. he said that the strong is not the one who overcomes people by his strength, but the one who controls himself while in anger. self-mastery is the muscle that matters, and it grows the way every muscle grows: through repetition at a weight you can lift. fast on mondays. rise in the last part of the night once a week. have the difficult conversation this week, not next month.

the man who walked past

bilal did not free himself. he was lying under the rock when abu bakr walked by, objected, and paid. umar ibn khattab, radiyallahu anhu, would often say, "abu bakr is our master and he freed our master," meaning bilal.

we love the story of the lone hero because it flatters us. the real story has a witness in it. one man saw another man suffering, refused to keep walking, and changed the ending.

find your abu bakr, and be someone's. tell a friend what you are building and ask them to ask you about it on friday. a hard thing done in front of a witness is a different thing from a hard thing done in private.

what to do this week

  • write the prize. one sentence: this is worth the cost because ____.
  • choose your word. a short cue that points to your reason.
  • make an if-then plan. after [something you already do], i will [one small action], every day.
  • do one chosen hard thing. small, planned, finishable.
  • tell one person. ask them to check on you on friday.
  • give it time. weeks to months, with a wide range between people.

bilal was a slave who became the voice that called the believers to prayer. he got there the way people get anywhere that matters: one word under pressure, one rule kept every day, and one friend who would not walk past.

may Allah be pleased with him, and make us people who stand up when it is hard.
