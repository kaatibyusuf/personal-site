---
title: abdullah ibn umar taught me discipline
date: 2026-11-27
summary: lessons from the life of abdullah ibn umar on how to build a steady, disciplined life.
---
about eight years ago, i was fortunate to read the story of abdullah ibn umar, radiyallahu anhuma, the son of umar ibn khattab. it stayed with me, and i keep coming back to it.

before reading that story, i had a schedule i followed, thanks to my dad. it was sustainable but i knew i needed something bigger to fit my growing self.

then i raed the story of abdullah ibn umar, and i realised his discipline was a gem. nobody wrote about his schedule. what they wrote about was how he *lived*, and slowly, across a very long life, it added up to something heavy and beautiful.

so this is what i'm learning from him. take it slowly, like i'm trying to.

## he was turned back, and he stayed ready

when the army marched out for uhud, abdullah was fourteen. he came forward to join and the prophet ﷺ did not allow him. imagine being a boy of that age, son of umar, wanting nothing more than to stand with the muslims, and being sent back.

he did not sulk. a year or so later at the trench, he presented himself again. he was fifteen, and this time he was accepted.

i think about how many times i've been told "not yet" and let it turn into "never". a closed door made me bitter. for him it just meant: wait, and be ready. discipline starts here. it's not doing big things. it's staying prepared for the door to open, and not letting the waiting spoil you.

## one sentence, and he changed his nights

this is the story i come back to most. abdullah used to sleep in the mosque in the prophet's time, and in those days when someone had a dream he would tell it to the prophet ﷺ. abdullah dreamed that two angels carried him toward the fire, and then another angel met them and told him not to be afraid. his sister hafsah told the prophet ﷺ about it.

the prophet's reply is what gets me. he said: what an excellent man abdullah is, if only he would pray at night.

look at the order. first praise, then the one thing missing. he didn't say "you're lazy". he said "you are excellent, and there is one gap".

and then salim, his son, says: after that, abdullah slept very little at night.

hmmmmm.... he didn't say "i'll start next month" or "my situation is different". he took the one gap that was pointed out to him and closed it, for the rest of his life. and the thing he closed it with was not a long list of rules. it was one habit: standing in the dark, alone, with his lord.

when i look at my own life, there's almost always one gap that i already know about. one thing i've been told, or told myself, many times. discipline isn't fixing ten things. it's finally fixing that one.

## eight years on one surah

there is a report in the muwatta of imam malik that it took abdullah ibn umar eight years to learn sūratul-baqarah.

eight years. for a single surah, from a man who sat with the prophet ﷺ himself. it's reported that he wasn't slow. he was learning it the way the companions learned the quran: a few verses, then what they say, then what they ask of you, then doing it, then the next few.

we live in a time of finishing fast. finish the book, finish the course, finish the series. and i'm guilty of it. i collect beginnings. his way was the opposite. stay with one thing until it has changed you, and only then move on.

i've started asking myself one question when i pick up a new thing: am i going to live with this long enough for it to change how i act? if not, maybe i'm only collecting.

## loving the footsteps

it's reported that abdullah ibn umar followed the prophet's footsteps with a love that people noticed. if the prophet ﷺ had stopped at a place on a journey, he would stop there. where the prophet had prayed, he would pray. some of the companions even found it a lot, and not everyone did what he did.

i don't think this is about copying small details for the sake of it. i think it's about what love does to discipline. when you love someone, you pay attention to what they did, even the small things. and love doesn't get tired the way willpower does. willpower gets you through a month. love can get you through sixty years.

this changed how i look at my habits. when something is only a rule, i resent it. when it's tied to someone i love, it becomes light. so now i try to attach each habit to a reason that actually moves me, not just "i should".

## a stranger, a traveler

once the prophet ﷺ took abdullah by the shoulder and told him: be in this world as if you were a stranger or a traveler.

and abdullah took it so seriously that he used to say: if you reach the evening, don't wait for the morning. if you reach the morning, don't wait for the evening. take from your health for your sickness, and from your life for your death.

that last part, take from your health for your sickness, is discipline in one sentence. use your good days. the days when you have energy, when your mind is clear, when nothing is on fire, those are not for coasting. those are the days you store up for the hard ones.

when i'm well, i feel like i've got forever. then i fall sick, or something happens, and i wish i had done more with the days when i could. a traveler doesn't unpack his whole life at a train station. he moves with what matters. he keeps his bag light and his eyes on where he's going.

## saying no

this part is quieter, but i think it's the most grown-up.

it's reported that when uthman radiyallahu anhu asked him to take the position of judge, he refused. he was known for being careful about giving rulings, and for saying he didn't know when he didn't. and when the muslims were torn apart by the civil strife after uthman's death, he kept himself out of the fighting and was careful with what he said.

i'll leave the debates about that to the scholars. what i see is a man who could say no. he had every kind of standing: son of umar, close to the prophet's family, a knowledgeable companion. and he didn't grab every opportunity that came. he knew what he could carry and what would drag him away from his lord.

most of my wasted days were not wasted on bad things. they were wasted on good-looking things i said yes to without thinking. more projects, more people to please, more things to be seen doing. every "yes" costs something, and it's usually the time that was meant for what matters most. saying "i don't know", "not now", or "no" is also discipline.

## wealth he didn't let own him

it's reported that he gave a lot away, and that he freed many slaves, and that if he found something he loved in his own hands he would give it for the sake of Allah. people say he rarely ate unless there were poor people or orphans eating with him.

this is a man who had means. and what struck me is that he treated his wealth the way he treated his time, as something passing through him.

this one i find hardest. i want to be disciplined in the sense of control. but he teaches that real discipline over your desires is also loosening your grip. you can't keep a hand open and a hand closed at the same time. every time i give something i love, i'm practising for the day it all has to be given back anyway.

## the school he came from

he didn't come from nowhere. he was raised in the house of umar, and umar is attributed with the saying: hold yourselves to account before you are held to account.

that's the root of it all. there's no discipline without the habit of looking at yourself honestly each day. not to beat yourself up. to see. what did i do well, where did i slip, what will i fix tomorrow. a merchant checks his books every evening. a believer should check his days.

i've started doing this at night, three questions, no more. what did i do today for my lord, what did i do for the people i love, and what am i going to change tomorrow. it takes five minutes. it has changed more than any schedule.

## what i'm keeping from him

if i had to write it small, for myself:

- when you're turned back, don't get bitter. get ready.
- find the one gap you already know about, and close it.
- stay with one thing until it changes you.
- tie your habits to love, not only to rules.
- use your strong days for your hard days.
- learn to say no, and "i don't know".
- hold your wealth and your time with an open hand.
- count yourself every night.

abdullah ibn umar lived a very long life, into his eighties, and passed away in makkah. nobody remembers him for one big dramatic moment. they remember him because he was steady, for decades, in a way people could see and feel.

and that gives me hope. i'm not going to become disciplined in a day. but i can be a little more steady than yesterday. and so can you.

may Allah be pleased with him, and with us, and make us people who start small and don't stop.
